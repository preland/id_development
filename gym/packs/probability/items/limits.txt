concepts: linearity
kind: solve
answer_kind: integer
difficulty: 850
transfer: no
verified: tool
prompt: Two fair dice are rolled. What is the expected value of their sum? Give a whole number.
answer: 7
rubric: Uses E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] instead of the distribution of the sum.
rubric: Uses 3.5 for one die.
hint_nudge: You do not need the distribution of the sum.
hint_question: What is the expected value of one die, and how does the expected value of a sum relate to the individual expected values?
hint_step: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]. Use the expected value of a single fair die twice.
hint_example: E[one die] = 3.5, so E[sum] = 3.5 + 3.5 = 7.

prompt: Five people each leave a hat at a party. The hats are returned in a completely random order, one to each person. What is the expected number of people who get their own hat back? Give a whole number.
answer: 1
rubric: Defines one indicator per person.
rubric: Notes that each person has probability 1/5 and that no independence is needed.
hint_nudge: Count with indicator variables: one for each person, switched on when that person gets their own hat.
hint_question: For one particular person, what is the probability of getting their own hat back, and how many such people are there?
hint_step: E[number of matches] is the sum over the 5 people of P(that person gets their own hat). Each term is the same.
hint_example: Each person has probability 1/5 of getting their own hat. By linearity, E = 5 x 1/5 = 1. This holds for any number of people.

answer: 5/13
rubric: Uses one indicator per dealt position even though the cards are dependent.
rubric: Gives each position probability 4/52.

hint_question: For one particular position among the 5 dealt cards, what is the probability it holds an ace?

item: large_numbers_1
concepts: large_numbers
kind: choice
answer_kind: choice
difficulty: 800
transfer: no
verified: source
prompt: A fair coin is flipped 10,000 times. Which quantity is most likely to be very close to its "expected" value? A) The number of heads. B) The proportion of flips that are heads. C) The difference between the number of heads and the number of tails. D) The longest streak of heads.
answer: B
rubric: Distinguishes a proportion (an average) from a raw count or difference.
hint_nudge: The law of large numbers is about averages, not about raw totals.
hint_question: Which of the four quantities is an average or a proportion?
hint_step: Compare how each quantity behaves as the number of flips grows: totals and differences can drift further from zero, while an average settles down.
hint_example: The proportion of heads gets closer and closer to 1/2. The count of heads typically misses 5,000 by dozens, and the heads-minus-tails difference typically grows with the square root of the number of flips. So the answer is B.

item: large_numbers_3
concepts: large_numbers
kind: error_hunt
answer_kind: choice
difficulty: 950
transfer: no
verified: source
prompt: A student flips a fair coin 10 times and gets 7 heads. She says: "By the law of large numbers, my next 10 flips will have about 3 more tails than heads, to balance this out." What is wrong? A) The law says averages approach the expected value as trials pile up; it does not make later flips compensate for earlier ones. B) The coin must be unfair. C) Ten flips is too many for the law to apply. D) Nothing is wrong.
answer: A
rubric: States that later independent flips do not compensate for an early surplus.
rubric: Says the proportion converges because the surplus is diluted, not cancelled.
hint_nudge: Ask whether the later flips even know about the earlier ones.
hint_question: Are the next 10 flips independent of the first 10, and what does the law of large numbers actually claim?
hint_step: The law concerns the long-run proportion. Ask what happens to the effect of an early surplus as the total number of flips grows.
hint_example: Later flips are independent and fair, so they average 5 heads in 10. The early surplus of 2 heads is not cancelled; it just becomes a smaller fraction of a much bigger total, so the proportion drifts toward 1/2. The mistake is described by option A.

answer: 69

rubric: Counts 5% of the other 980 as false positives.
hint_nudge: Split the 1000 people into those with and those without the condition.
hint_question: How many have the condition, how many do not, and how many from each group test positive?

item: fallacies_4
concepts: fallacies
kind: choice
answer_kind: choice
difficulty: 850
transfer: no
verified: source
prompt: A wheel is spun repeatedly; each spin is independent and red is as likely as black. After six reds in a row, a gambler bets on black because "black is due". Which statement is correct? A) Black is now more likely than red on the next spin. B) Red is now more likely than black on the next spin. C) Black and red are exactly as likely on the next spin as they always were. D) The wheel must be broken.
answer: C
rubric: Applies independence: past spins do not change the next one.
hint_nudge: Check what the problem says about the spins being independent.
hint_question: If spins are independent, can the earlier six spins change the probability of the next one?
hint_step: Independent trials have no memory. Decide how the next spin's probabilities compare with those of any other spin.
hint_example: Independent spins do not remember the past, so the probabilities for the next spin are the same as for every spin. Option C is correct; "black is due" is the gambler's fallacy.

item: fallacies_transfer
concepts: fallacies, bayes_rule
kind: solve
answer_kind: fraction
difficulty: 1300
transfer: yes
verified: tool
prompt: In a city, 85% of cabs are green and 15% are blue. A witness says a cab in an accident was blue. The witness correctly identifies the colour of a cab 80% of the time, and is wrong 20% of the time. What is the probability that the cab really was blue? Give a fraction in lowest terms.
answer: 12/29
rubric: Uses the 15% base rate of blue cabs.
rubric: Adds the false-blue reports from green cabs to the denominator.
hint_nudge: Do not forget how many cabs of each colour there are.
hint_question: What fraction of all cabs are blue AND described as blue, and what fraction are green but described as blue?
hint_step: P(blue | says blue) = P(blue and says blue) / [P(blue and says blue) + P(green and says blue)]. Compute both products.
hint_example: Blue and called blue: 0.15 x 0.8 = 0.12. Green but called blue: 0.85 x 0.2 = 0.17. So P = 0.12 / (0.12 + 0.17) = 12/29.
