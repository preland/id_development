prompt: Two fair coins are flipped. What is the probability that both land heads? Give a fraction.
answer: 1/4
rubric: Multiplies the two independent probabilities.
hint_nudge: The result of one coin has no influence on the other.
hint_question: What is the probability that the first coin lands heads, and what is it for the second?
hint_step: For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) times P(B). Multiply the two probabilities.
hint_example: P(first heads) = 1/2 and P(second heads) = 1/2, so P(both) = 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4.

prompt: A fair die is rolled twice. Let A be "the first roll is even" and B be "the two rolls add up to 7". Are A and B independent? Answer yes or no.
answer: yes
rubric: Computes P(A), P(B) and P(A and B) separately.
rubric: Compares P(A and B) with P(A) x P(B).
hint_nudge: Independent means P(A and B) = P(A) times P(B); you need all three numbers.
hint_question: What are P(A) and P(B), and how many of the 36 pairs make both events true?
hint_step: Compute P(A), P(B) and P(A and B) separately by counting pairs, then compare P(A and B) with the product P(A) x P(B).
hint_example: P(A) = 18/36 = 1/2. P(B) = 6/36 = 1/6. Both: first even and sum 7 gives (2,5), (4,3), (6,1), so P(A and B) = 3/36 = 1/12. Since 1/2 x 1/6 = 1/12, the events are independent: yes.

prompt: Two fair dice are rolled. Given that the sum is at least 10, what is the probability that both dice show the same number? Give a fraction in lowest terms.
answer: 1/3
rubric: Lists the six pairs with sum 10, 11 or 12.
rubric: Counts the two doubles among them.
hint_nudge: Narrow down to the pairs whose sum is at least 10 before counting anything else.
hint_question: List the pairs (first die, second die) with sum 10, 11 or 12. How many are there, and how many of them are doubles?
hint_step: Use P(A | B) = (outcomes in both A and B) / (outcomes in B), with B = "sum at least 10" and A = "both dice equal".
hint_example: Sum 10: (4,6), (5,5), (6,4). Sum 11: (5,6), (6,5). Sum 12: (6,6). That is 6 pairs. Doubles among them: (5,5) and (6,6), which is 2. So P = 2/6 = 1/3.

prompt: A student writes P(rain | cloudy) = P(rain and cloudy) / P(rain). Which denominator is correct? A) P(rain) is correct as written. B) P(cloudy) C) P(not cloudy) D) 1
answer: B

hint_question: In P(A | B), which event is known to have happened, and what is the size of that world?

hint_example: In P(rain | cloudy) the given event is "cloudy", so the world is "cloudy" and the denominator must be P(cloudy). The correct formula is P(rain and cloudy) / P(cloudy). The answer is option B.

item: bayes_1
concepts: bayes_rule
kind: solve
answer_kind: fraction
difficulty: 1100
transfer: no
verified: tool
prompt: In a population, 1% of people have a certain condition. A test is positive for 90% of people who have it and also positive for 10% of people who do not. A randomly chosen person tests positive. What is the probability that they have the condition? Give a fraction in lowest terms.
answer: 1/12
rubric: Computes true positives and false positives from the prior and the two rates.
rubric: Divides true positives by all positives.
hint_nudge: Imagine a concrete group of 1000 people and follow each part of it through the test.
hint_question: Out of 1000 people, how many have the condition and test positive, and how many do not have it but still test positive?
hint_step: P(condition | positive) = (true positives) / (all positives). Count both kinds of positives in the group of 1000 and divide.
hint_example: Of 1000 people, 10 have the condition and 90% of them test positive: 9. The 990 others test positive 10% of the time: 99. Positives: 9 + 99 = 108. So P = 9/108 = 1/12.

item: bayes_2
concepts: bayes_rule
kind: solve
answer_kind: fraction
difficulty: 1000
transfer: no
verified: tool
prompt: Bag A holds 3 red balls and 1 blue ball. Bag B holds 1 red ball and 3 blue balls. A bag is chosen at random with equal probability, and one ball is drawn from it. The ball is red. What is the probability that it came from Bag A? Give a fraction.
answer: 3/4
rubric: Gives P(red given A) = 3/4 and P(red given B) = 1/4.
rubric: Divides the Bag A contribution by the total probability of red.
hint_nudge: Before drawing, the bags are equally likely; the red ball is evidence that shifts that.
hint_question: What is the probability of drawing red from each bag, and what is the total probability of drawing red?
hint_step: P(A | red) = P(red | A) P(A) / P(red), with P(red) = P(red | A) P(A) + P(red | B) P(B).
hint_example: P(red | A) = 3/4 and P(red | B) = 1/4, and each bag has probability 1/2. P(red) = 3/8 + 1/8 = 1/2. So P(A | red) = (3/8) / (1/2) = 3/4.

item: bayes_3
concepts: bayes_rule
kind: solve
answer_kind: percent
difficulty: 1150
transfer: no
verified: tool
prompt: A factory has two machines. Machine 1 makes 60% of the items and 2% of its items are defective. Machine 2 makes 40% of the items and 5% of its items are defective. An item is chosen at random and found to be defective. What is the probability, as a percentage, that it came from Machine 1?
answer: 37.5%
rubric: Computes the fraction of all items that are defective and from Machine 1.
rubric: Divides by the total fraction of defective items.
hint_nudge: Work out what fraction of ALL items are defective and come from each machine.
hint_question: What fraction of all items are both from Machine 1 and defective, and what fraction of all items are defective?
hint_step: P(M1 | defective) = P(M1 and defective) / P(defective), where P(defective) adds the contribution of both machines.
hint_example: M1 and defective: 0.6 x 0.02 = 0.012. M2 and defective: 0.4 x 0.05 = 0.020. Total defective: 0.032. So P(M1 | defective) = 0.012 / 0.032 = 0.375 = 37.5%.

item: bayes_4
concepts: bayes_rule
kind: error_hunt
answer_kind: choice
difficulty: 950
transfer: no

prompt: A student says: "My test for a rare disease (1 person in 1000 has it) is 95% accurate. I tested positive, so there is a 95% chance I have the disease." What has the student overlooked? A) How rare the disease is in the first place (the base rate). B) The test's accuracy, which should be 100%. C) The number of people tested. D) Nothing; the reasoning is correct.
answer: A
rubric: Names the base rate (prior probability) as the missing ingredient.
rubric: Explains that most positives are false alarms when the disease is rare.
hint_nudge: Compare how many people have the disease with how many people the test might wrongly flag.
hint_question: In a group of 1000 people, roughly how many have the disease, and roughly how many healthy people would test positive?
hint_step: Think about all the people who test positive, and ask what share of them are actually sick.
hint_example: Of 1000 people about 1 has the disease, but 5% of the 999 healthy people, about 50, also test positive. So most positives are false alarms and the chance of being sick is small, not 95%. The student ignored the base rate: option A.

item: bayes_transfer
concepts: bayes_rule
kind: solve
answer_kind: fraction
difficulty: 1250
transfer: yes
verified: tool
prompt: 20% of incoming emails are spam. The word "prize" appears in 50% of spam emails and in 5% of emails that are not spam. An email contains the word "prize". What is the probability that it is spam? Give a fraction in lowest terms.
answer: 5/7
rubric: Computes P(prize and spam) = 0.10 and P(prize and not spam) = 0.04.
rubric: Divides the first by their sum.
hint_nudge: Compare how often "prize" shows up from spam with how often it shows up from legitimate mail, weighted by how common each kind of mail is.
hint_question: What fraction of all emails are spam that contain "prize", and what fraction are non-spam that contain it?
hint_step: P(spam | prize) = P(prize and spam) / [P(prize and spam) + P(prize and not spam)]. Compute the two products and put them in the fraction.
hint_example: Spam with "prize": 0.2 x 0.5 = 0.10. Non-spam with "prize": 0.8 x 0.05 = 0.04. So P(spam | prize) = 0.10 / 0.14 = 5/7.
